Can an 8-qubit quantum circuit compete with a classical neural network when both are given the exact same number of trainable parameters? We tested both side by side across 10 random seeds on real astronomical survey data.

01 / THE CHALLENGE

Finding a Cosmic Lighthouse in a Sea of Static

A pulsar is the collapsed core of a giant star. It spins rapidly and sweeps beams of radio waves across space like a lighthouse. When a beam crosses Earth, radio telescopes record a faint pulse.

The catch is noise. Over 90% of recorded signals come from satellites, radar, and everyday interference. Because real pulsars make up less than a tenth of the data, standard accuracy is misleading: a model that guesses “noise” every time still scores above 90% while missing every star. Instead, we grade every model with PR-AUC, a single score from 0.00 to 1.00 that balances two questions: when the model flags a pulsar, is it right, and how many real pulsars did it catch?

02 / A FAIR TEST

41 Parameters vs. 41 Parameters

Many quantum machine learning comparisons pit a tuned quantum circuit against an oversized classical network, or the reverse. We removed that bias. Each radio signal contains 8 physical measurements, which map one-to-one onto 8 qubits with no lossy compression. We then matched a variational quantum circuit (Q1-VQC) against a compact classical neural network (Tiny-MLP) with 41 trainable weights apiece, isolating architecture from raw model size.     05 / THE TAKEAWAY

    No Hype, Just Clear Boundaries

    An 8-qubit system (28 = 256 states) runs easily on a laptop, so we claim no hardware speedup today. What the benchmarks show is more practical. Lightweight classical networks remain the best default for well-separated tabular data. Compact quantum circuits add measurable value when labels are scarce, when circuit depth is kept shallow (8 two-qubit gates instead of 16), or when signals follow curved, nonlinear patterns.
